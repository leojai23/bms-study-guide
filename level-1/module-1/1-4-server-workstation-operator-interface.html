Module 1 · BMS Architecture

1.4 BMS server, workstation and operator interface

Week 1 · Day 3About 10 minPage 4 of 6✓ Done
After this page you can
  • Explain what the BMS server does and what it stores.
  • Describe the four screens an operator uses most: graphics, alarms, trends and schedules.
  • Follow the life cycle of an alarm.
  • Explain why manual overrides must be managed carefully.
Warm-up · 3 quick questions from earlier pages (2 min)
  1. Name three things a controller stores in its memory.
    from 1.3 The DDC controller and how it works
    Show answer
    The program, settings such as setpoints and schedules, and recent trend and alarm data.
  2. Name the three levels of a BMS and give one device at each.
    from 1.2 BMS architecture: field, automation and management levels
    Show answer
    Management — BMS server or workstation. Automation — DDC plant controller or supervisory controller. Field — temperature sensor, valve actuator, VFD.
  3. Explain sense → decide → act using a room that is too warm.
    from 1.1 What is a BMS?
    Show answer
    The sensor measures 26 °C (sense). The controller compares it with the 24 °C setpoint, sees the room is too warm, and works out a bigger valve opening (decide). The actuator opens the chilled-water valve (act). The room cools, the sensor measures the new temperature, and the loop repeats.
In one line

The BMS server collects data from every controller and stores the building's history; the workstation and operator interface are how people see that data and give commands.

?Why it matters

Most people judge a BMS by what they see on screen. As a BMS engineer you will build these screens, set up the alarms and trends, and train the operators. Good front-end work turns thousands of points into a few clear pictures; poor work hides the one alarm that matters among a hundred that don't.

⚙How it works

The BMS server

The server is a computer — physical or virtual — running the BMS supervisory software. Its jobs:

FunctionWhat it means
Data collectionPolls or receives values from all controllers.
DatabaseHolds the list of every device, point and graphic.
History (trend logs)Stores values over time — e.g. AHU-01 return air temperature every 5 minutes for a year.
Alarm managementReceives, stores and routes alarms; sends e-mail or SMS notifications.
Schedules and calendarsA central place to view and edit schedules and holidays. The schedules themselves still run in the controllers.
Users and securityUser accounts, access levels, and an audit trail of who changed what.
IntegrationDrivers for third-party systems; exports to energy or maintenance software.
ReportsEnergy use, run-hours, alarm statistics, performance.

Workstations and clients

A workstation is a PC that operators use to work with the BMS. Most modern systems are web-based, so any authorised browser, tablet or phone can act as a client. Plant rooms may also have a local display (HMI) on the panel door, which shows the controller's points even when the server is unavailable.

The four screens every operator uses

Graphics

Pictures of the plant and floor plans with live values. Click a point to command it.

Alarm console

A list of active alarms with priority, time and state. This is where alarms are acknowledged.

Trends

Graphs of values over time — the best tool for finding when and why a problem started.

Schedules

Weekly time programs and holiday calendars that decide when plant runs.

The life of an alarm

Alarms carry a priority so the important ones stand out and can be routed differently:

PriorityExampleTypical routing
CriticalFire shutdown active, chiller plant trippedAlarm console + SMS to the on-call engineer
HighAHU fan failed, pump trippedAlarm console + e-mail
MediumRoom temperature out of range for 15 minutesAlarm console
Low / maintenanceFilter dirty, run-hours due for serviceAlarm console or maintenance report
Avoid alarm flooding

A good alarm is one somebody needs to act on. If operators see hundreds a day, they stop reading them. Use time delays (e.g. 15 minutes before a high-temperature alarm), suppress alarms that cannot matter (no temperature alarms while the AHU is off by schedule), and review alarm counts every month.

Commands and overrides

Operators can command points: start a fan, change a setpoint, force a valve to 100 %. A manual override replaces the program's value until someone releases it.

Forgotten overrides

Overrides left in place are one of the most common causes of wasted energy and “mystery” faults. A fan forced ON during a test runs all night; a valve forced to 0 % leaves a floor hot for weeks. Use timed overrides where the system allows it, and check the override list regularly.

Users and access levels

LevelTypical rights
View onlySee graphics, alarms and trends.
OperatorAcknowledge alarms; change setpoints and schedules within limits.
EngineerChange configuration, logic, graphics and alarm limits.
AdministratorManage users, backups and system settings.

Basic cyber-security: change every default password, give each person their own login (so the audit trail means something), keep the BMS network separate from the office network, and keep backups off the server.

Follow AHU-01

On the server, AHU-01 has:

  • a graphic like the one above;
  • alarms — fan fail (high), fire shutdown (critical), return air above 27 °C for 15 minutes (medium), filter dirty (low);
  • trends — return and supply air temperature, valve position, fan speed and CO₂, every 5 minutes;
  • a schedule — Monday to Saturday, 08:00–19:00, with the public-holiday calendar.

⚒On site

  • Make sure the server and all controllers use one time source (NTP); otherwise trends and alarm times will not line up.
  • When investigating a problem, open a trend before touching anything. It usually shows when the problem started and what changed.
  • Keep graphics honest: show units, put each setpoint next to the value it controls, and make points in override stand out.
  • Back up the server before any upgrade or major change, and store a copy somewhere other than the server.

AKey terms

Server
Computer running the BMS supervisory software and database.
Workstation / client
A PC, browser or mobile device used to operate the BMS.
HMI / local display
A screen on or near the panel showing the controller's points.
Graphic

✓Check yourself

Try each one before opening the answer.

Fault scenario · what would you do?

AHU-01 ran all weekend, although its schedule is Monday to Saturday 08:00–19:00 and the schedule is set correctly. What do you check first?

A correct schedule that is not being followed usually means the program's output is being overridden. Check the override list (and the HOA switch in Hand). Then release the override and find out why it was set.

  1. Name four functions of a BMS server.

    Show answer
    Any four of: data collection, database, history/trends, alarm management, schedules and calendars, users and security, integration, reports.

  2. An alarm returns to normal before anyone acknowledges it. Does it leave the alarm list?

    Show answer
    No. It moves to “returned to normal, unacknowledged” and stays on the list until an operator acknowledges it, so short events are not missed.

  3. Give two ways to reduce nuisance alarms.

    Show answer
    Add time delays; suppress dependent alarms when plant is off by schedule; set sensible limits; review alarm counts regularly.

  4. Why should every operator have their own login?

    Show answer
    So the audit trail shows who made each change, and access rights can be set per person.

  5. Why are forgotten overrides a problem?

    Show answer
    They keep equipment in a fixed state regardless of the program — wasting energy or causing comfort complaints — often without anyone noticing.
