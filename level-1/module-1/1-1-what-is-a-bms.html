Module 1 · BMS Architecture

1.1 What is a BMS?

Week 1 · Day 1About 10 minPage 1 of 6✓ Done
After this page you can
  • Explain in plain words what a BMS is — and what it is not.
  • List the five jobs every BMS does.
  • Name the building systems a BMS controls, and those it only monitors.
  • Describe the basic control loop: sense → decide → act.
In one line

A Building Management System (BMS) is a network of controllers and software that monitors and automatically controls a building's services — mainly air-conditioning and ventilation — from one central place.

?Why it matters

HVAC is usually the largest energy user in a commercial building, and it runs every day. Without automation, someone would have to walk the plant rooms switching equipment on and off, adjusting valves and checking for faults. A BMS does that work continuously: it keeps people comfortable, cuts wasted energy, and tells the maintenance team about a problem before the occupants notice it.

Every BMS task you will do — wiring a sensor, programming a controller, integrating a chiller — serves those three goals: comfort, energy and reliability.

⚙How it works

Other names you will hear

TermMeaning
BMSBuilding Management System — the usual term in India, the UK, the Middle East and most of Asia.
BASBuilding Automation System — the usual term in the USA. Same thing.
BACSBuilding Automation and Control System — the term used in the ISO 16484 and EN standards.
BEMSBuilding Energy Management System — a BMS with the emphasis on energy metering and reporting.
IBMSIntegrated BMS — one platform that also brings in fire alarm, CCTV, access control and more.

This guide uses BMS throughout.

The five jobs of a BMS

  1. Monitor — read values from sensors and equipment: temperatures, pressures, run status, faults.
  2. Control — decide what equipment should do and send commands: start a fan, open a valve to 40 %, change a fan's speed.
  3. Alarm — compare values with limits and alert people when something is wrong: a fan has tripped, a room is too hot, a filter is blocked.
  4. Schedule — run equipment only when it is needed, for example office AHUs from 08:00 to 19:00 on working days.
  5. Record and report — store values over time (trends) and produce energy and performance reports.

The basic idea: sense → decide → act

Every automatic control in a building — from one fan coil unit to a whole chiller plant — follows the same three-step loop.

The value the controller is trying to reach is the setpoint (24 °C). The value it measures is the process variable (26 °C). Because the sensor measures the result of the action, the controller keeps correcting until the two match. This is called closed-loop or feedback control.

What a BMS controls — and what it only watches

SystemTypical BMS role
HVAC — AHUs, FCUs, chillers, pumps, cooling towers, ventilation fansFull monitoring and control. This is the core of every BMS.

Life-safety systems stay independent

A BMS never replaces the fire alarm panel or fire-fighting controls. Safety actions — such as stopping an AHU when the fire alarm operates — are hard-wired so they work even if the BMS fails. You will meet this again in Module 6.

Follow AHU-01

Throughout Level 1 we follow one example: AHU-01, an air handling unit that cools the 3rd-floor offices of a commercial building. It has a supply fan on a VFD, a chilled-water cooling coil with a 2-way valve, a filter, a modulating fresh air damper and a manual return air damper.

Its BMS job: hold the offices at 24 °C from 08:00 to 19:00, Monday to Saturday, and raise an alarm if anything fails. Each page adds one piece of the picture. See the full schematic on the Level 1 overview.

⚒On site

  • On a new site, ask first for the BMS architecture drawing and the points list. Together they tell you what the system does.
  • On project drawings, “BMS” usually means the whole package: controllers, panels, sensors, cables, software and integration.
  • Before writing any logic that touches a life-safety system, check the interface schedule — the BMS usually only monitors it.
  • Operators often see only the graphics. Learn to trace a value on screen back to the physical sensor; it is the core troubleshooting skill.

AKey terms

BMS / BAS
A computer-based system that monitors and controls a building's services.
Point
One value in the BMS: an input (e.g. a temperature) or an output (e.g. a start command).
Setpoint
The target value the controller is trying to reach, e.g. 24 °C.
Process variable
The measured value being controlled, e.g. the actual room temperature.
Closed-loop control
Control where the result is measured and fed back, so the controller keeps correcting.
Trend
A record of a point's value over time, shown as a graph.
Alarm
A notification that a value is outside its limits or equipment has failed.
Schedule
A time program that decides when equipment should run.

✓Check yourself

Try each one before opening the answer.

Fault scenario · what would you do?

A facility manager asks you to “make the BMS start the fire pumps when a fire is detected”. What is the right response?

Life-safety systems must work even if the BMS fails, so they control themselves and are tested under fire regulations. The BMS monitors fire pump run, fault and power status and raises alarms.

  1. What are the five jobs of a BMS?

    Show answer
    Monitor, control, alarm, schedule, and record/report.

  2. Explain sense → decide → act using a room that is too warm.

    Show answer
    The sensor measures 26 °C (sense). The controller compares it with the 24 °C setpoint, sees the room is too warm, and works out a bigger valve opening (decide). The actuator opens the chilled-water valve (act). The room cools, the sensor measures the new temperature, and the loop repeats.

  3. Why is the fire alarm system kept independent of the BMS?

    Show answer
    Life-safety functions must work even if the BMS, its network or its software fails. Fire systems have their own approved controls; the BMS only receives signals for monitoring, and safety actions such as AHU shutdown are hard-wired.

  4. What is the difference between a setpoint and a process variable?

    Show answer
    The setpoint is the target (24 °C). The process variable is the measured actual value (e.g. 25.2 °C). The controller acts on the difference between them, called the error.

  5. Name three systems a BMS typically only monitors.

    Show answer
    Any three of: generators (DG), UPS, transformers and electrical panels, lifts, the fire alarm system, energy meters.
