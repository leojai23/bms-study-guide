2.7 HVAC control sequences — an overview

Warm-up · 3 quick questions from earlier pages (2 min)
  1. What is blowdown and why is it needed?
    from 2.6 Cooling tower and condenser water
    Show answer
    Draining part of the tower water so that minerals concentrated by evaporation do not build up into scale and corrosion.
  2. What is low ΔT syndrome and why is it costly?
    from 2.5 Chilled water system and chiller plant
    Show answer
    Chilled water returning too cold. Each chiller can only deliver part of its capacity, so more chillers and pumps run than the load needs.
  3. What varies in a CAV system and what varies in a VAV system?
    from 2.4 VAV and CAV systems
    Show answer
    CAV: airflow is fixed; supply temperature varies. VAV: supply temperature is roughly constant; airflow to each zone varies.
In one line

A sequence of operation is the written, step-by-step description of exactly how a piece of plant must start, run, stop and react to faults — the specification the BMS program is built from and tested against.

?Why it matters

The program in a controller is only as good as the sequence behind it. A clear sequence lets the designer, the programmer, the commissioning engineer and the operator all agree on what the plant should do. A vague one (“the AHU shall be controlled as required”) leads to arguments, rework and buildings that never quite work.

⚙How it works

What a complete sequence covers

SectionAnswers the question
Equipment and pointsWhat plant and which points are involved?
Operating modesOccupied, unoccupied, fire, maintenance, hand — what changes in each?
Enable conditions (permissives)What must be true before it may start?
Start sequenceIn what order do things happen, with what delays and proofs?
Normal controlWhich loops run, to which setpoints, with what limits?
Stop sequenceIn what order does it shut down?
Interlocks and safetiesWhat must stop it, regardless of the program?
AlarmsWhat is alarmed, with what limits, delays and priorities?
Failure responsesWhat happens if a sensor fails, communication is lost or power returns?
Adjustable parametersWhich setpoints and timers can an operator change, and within what limits?

Operating modes

A short example: AHU-01

  1. Enable: when the schedule is Occupied (or an operator override is active), and the HOA is in Auto, and there is no fire signal or latched fan fault.
  2. Start: command the fresh air damper to minimum (20 %), then start the supply fan.
  3. Prove: if run status is not received within 30 s, raise “fan fail”, stop the fan and lock out until reset.
  4. Control: after 60 s, enable the PID loop holding return air at 24 °C (adjustable 22–26 °C) by modulating the CHW valve; modulate the fresh air damper on return CO₂.
  5. Stop: at the end of occupancy, close the valve, stop the fan, then close the fresh air damper.
  6. Fire: on a fire signal the fan stops immediately via the hard-wired trip; the BMS closes the valve and damper and raises a critical alarm.

Lab 3 turns this summary into a complete, testable sequence; page 6.1 explains every step.

How to write a good sequence

  • One action per step, in the order it happens.
  • Give numbers — setpoints, delays, limits — and mark them “adjustable”.
  • Say how each step is proven (“when run status is received”), not just what is commanded.
  • Say what happens when it goes wrong: failure to prove, sensor failure, communication loss, power return.
  • Use the point names from the points list, so programmer and tester mean the same thing.
  • Avoid “as required”, “as necessary”, “suitable” — they cannot be tested.

Standard sequences

ASHRAE Guideline 36 publishes standardised, high-performance sequences for AHUs and VAV terminal units. Even when a project does not adopt it, it is an excellent reference for how good sequences are written.

Follow AHU-01

The six steps above are AHU-01's sequence in summary. Over the next modules you will learn what is behind each one: the devices (Module 3), the wiring (Module 4), the communication (Module 5) and the logic (Module 6). In Lab 3 you write it out in full.

⚒On site

  • Get the approved sequence before programming; mark up any deviations and get them agreed.
  • During commissioning, test the sequence step by step and tick each one — including the failure steps.
  • Put the adjustable parameters (setpoints, delays) on the graphic so operators can see them.
  • Keep the final, as-built sequence with the O&M documents; the next engineer will need it.

AKey terms

Sequence of operation
Written step-by-step description of how plant must operate.
Permissive
A condition that must be true before plant may start.
Proving
Confirming that a commanded action actually happened, e.g. run status after a start command.
Operating mode
A defined state such as occupied, unoccupied, fire or hand.
Lockout
A state after a fault in which plant will not restart until reset.
Adjustable parameter
A setpoint or timer an operator can change within limits.
Staggered restart
Restarting plant items one after another after a power cut.

✓Check yourself

Try each one before opening the answer.

Fault scenario · what would you do?

Power returns after a grid failure. Within two seconds all 40 AHUs, 12 pumps and 4 chillers try to start. The generator trips on overload. What should the sequence have included?

Starting large motors one after another limits the inrush current. Sequences should define power-return behaviour: restart delays per item, and which plant restarts automatically.

  1. Why should every sequence step say how it is proven?

    Show answer
    Because a command does not guarantee the action happened. The sequence must say what feedback confirms each step (e.g. run status) and what happens if it does not arrive.

  2. Name five sections of a complete sequence of operation.

    Show answer
    Any five of: equipment and points, modes, enable conditions, start sequence, normal control, stop sequence, interlocks and safeties, alarms, failure responses, adjustable parameters.

  3. Which mode overrides every other mode?

    Show answer
    Fire mode.

  4. Rewrite “the valve shall be controlled as required”.

    Show answer
    For example: “When the supply fan is proven running, a PID loop shall modulate the CHW valve (0–100 %) to hold return air temperature at 24 °C (adjustable 22–26 °C). When the fan is off, the valve shall be closed.”

  5. What is ASHRAE Guideline 36?

    Show answer
    A published set of standardised high-performance control sequences for AHUs and VAV terminal units.
