6.3 PID control basics

In one line

A PID loop sets its output from the error (setpoint − measured value): P reacts to how big the error is, I to how long it has lasted, D to how fast it is changing.

?Why it matters

Nearly every modulating output in a BMS — valves, dampers, fan speeds — is driven by a PID loop. A badly tuned loop wears out actuators, wastes energy and causes comfort complaints that look like equipment faults. You do not need the maths to tune well; you need to know what each term does and what a good response looks like.

⚙How it works

Error and action

Error is the difference between setpoint and process variable. The action decides which way the output moves: a cooling valve must open more as the temperature rises above setpoint; a heating valve must open more as it falls below. Many products call these direct and reverse acting — but the naming is not consistent between manufacturers, so check the help and test it: if the loop drives the wrong way, the output runs straight to one end.

P — proportional

The output is proportional to the error. It is often set as a proportional band (PB): the error that moves the output across its whole range. A PB of 4 K means each 1 K of error moves the valve 25 %. (Gain = 100 % ÷ PB.)

Why P alone leaves an offset

To hold the valve at, say, 60 %, a P-only controller needs an error — 2.4 K with a 4 K band. So the temperature settles away from setpoint. That steady error is the offset. A narrower band shrinks it, but too narrow and the loop oscillates.

I — integral

Integral action keeps adding to the output for as long as any error remains, so it slowly pushes the error to zero and removes the offset. It is set as an integral time (Ti): shorter means faster — and more likely to overshoot.

Integral windup

If the output is stuck at a limit — valve at 100 % because the chilled water is too warm — the integral keeps growing. When conditions recover, the oversized integral holds the valve fully open long after it should close, causing a big overshoot. Controllers use anti-windup to stop integrating at the limits; loops should also be held at a sensible value while disabled (e.g. fan off) so they start smoothly.

D — derivative

Derivative reacts to how fast the measured value is changing, braking the output before the error gets large. It also amplifies noise. HVAC processes are slow and sensor signals noisy, so most HVAC loops use PI only (D = 0).

A safe way to tune an HVAC loop

  1. Make sure the loop is otherwise healthy: correct action, sensor reading correctly, actuator moving its full stroke, valve correctly sized.
  2. Start with P only, a wide band and no integral.
  3. Make a setpoint change and watch the trend. Narrow the band step by step until the response starts to oscillate, then widen it to about double that value.
  4. Add integral with a long Ti, then shorten it until the offset disappears in reasonable time without a large overshoot.
  5. Test at low and high load if you can — a loop tuned at full load can hunt at light load.
  6. Record the as-left settings.

Follow AHU-01

AHU-01's return air loop is PI: proportional band 4 K, integral time 240 s, no derivative, output limited to 0–100 % with anti-windup, held at 0 % while the fan is off. Try those values in the playground above — they are the “P + I (good)” preset — then try the aggressive and sluggish presets to see what badly tuned loops look like.

⚒On site

  • Always tune from a trend, not from a single reading.
  • Before touching PID settings, check the valve really moves: many “tuning problems” are mechanical.
  • Change one setting at a time and give the loop time to respond.
  • Note the original settings before changing them.

AKey terms

Error
Difference between setpoint and measured value.
Action
Direction in which the output moves for a given error (direct / reverse).
Proportional band
Error needed to move the output across its full range; gain = 100 % ÷ PB.
Offset
Steady error left by P-only control.
Integral time (Ti)
Setting of integral action; shorter is faster.
Windup
Integral building up while the output is at a limit, causing overshoot.
Derivative
Action based on the rate of change; rarely used in HVAC.
Hunting
Continuous oscillation around setpoint.
Overshoot
Going past the setpoint before settling.

Fault scenario · what would you do?

A new supply air temperature loop drives the CHW valve straight to 0 % at start-up and leaves it there, while the supply air is 20 °C against a 13 °C setpoint. What is the most likely cause?

Tuning makes a loop fast or slow; it does not make it go the wrong way. An output that runs to the wrong end and stays there is almost always the action setting (or the actuator's direction).

  1. A loop has a 5 K proportional band. How far does the output move for a 1 K error?

    Show answer
    20 % (100 % ÷ 5 K).

  2. Why does P-only control leave an offset?

    Show answer
    It needs an error to produce any output; to hold a non-zero output it must settle with a steady error.

  3. What does integral action do?

    Show answer
    Keeps adding to the output while any error remains, so it removes the offset over time.

  4. What is integral windup?

    Show answer
    The integral growing while the output is stuck at a limit, so the loop overshoots badly when conditions recover.

  5. Why is derivative rarely used in HVAC loops?

    Show answer
    HVAC processes are slow and sensors noisy; derivative amplifies noise and adds little benefit.
