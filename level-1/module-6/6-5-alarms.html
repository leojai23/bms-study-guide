6.5 Alarms: filter dirty, high and low temperature

Week 4 · Day 27About 10 minPage 5 of 6✓ Done
After this page you can

  • Set limits, deadbands, delays, enable conditions and priorities correctly.
  • Configure filter-dirty and high/low temperature alarms for an AHU.
  • Write alarm messages that tell the reader what to do.
Warm-up · 3 quick questions from earlier pages (2 min)
  1. What is cascade control?
    from 6.4 Temperature-based valve control and damper control
    Show answer
    An outer loop (room or return temperature) sets the setpoint of an inner loop (supply air), which drives the valve.
  2. What is integral windup?
    from 6.3 PID control basics
    Show answer
    The integral growing while the output is stuck at a limit, so the loop overshoots badly when conditions recover.
  3. Why are most safety trips latching?
    from 6.2 Fan interlocking and safeties
    Show answer
    So plant stays off until a person has found and fixed the cause, rather than cycling on and off into a fault.
In one line

A good alarm fires only when someone needs to act, clears cleanly, and tells the reader what is wrong, where, and what to check first.

?Why it matters

Alarms are how the BMS asks for help. Too few and failures go unnoticed; too many and operators stop reading them. The difference lies in a handful of settings — deadband, delay, enable condition, priority — and in how the message is written.

Filter dirty alarm

  1. Source: the filter ΔP switch (DI), or a ΔP sensor (AI) compared with the filter's recommended final pressure drop.
  2. Enable only while the fan has been proven running for 5 minutes — ΔP is meaningless with the fan off or starting.
  3. Delay 5 minutes so gusts and start-up transients do not trigger it.
  4. Priority low (maintenance) — the AHU keeps running.
  5. With a ΔP sensor, also trend it: the slow rise shows when filters will need changing.

Combine conditions for diagnosis

“Supply air high and valve fully open” tells the operator the AHU is doing its best and the problem is upstream. One well-designed combined alarm is worth several simple ones.

⚒On site

  • Review the alarm log weekly during the first months: anything frequent is either a real problem or a badly set alarm.
  • Test each alarm by creating the condition, and check it reaches the right person.
  • Agree priorities and routing with the facility team — they live with them.
  • Check “return to normal” works as well as the alarm itself.

AKey terms

Value at which the alarm condition starts.
Hysteresis (deadband)
Amount the value must return past the limit before the alarm clears.
Alarm delay
Time the condition must persist before the alarm is raised.

Condition under which an alarm is active.

✓Check yourself

Try each one before opening the answer.

Fault scenario · what would you do?

Operators complain that AHU-05's “return air high” alarm appears and clears 30 times an afternoon, with the value hovering around 27.0 °C. What should you change?

Without hysteresis and delay, a value hovering at the limit makes the alarm chatter. Fix the settings — and also look at why the AHU cannot hold 24 °C.

  1. What does an alarm deadband do?

    Show answer
    The value must return past the limit by the deadband before the alarm clears, so it does not chatter around the limit.

  2. Why is the filter-dirty alarm enabled only after the fan has run for 5 minutes?

    Show answer
    ΔP across the filter is meaningless when the fan is off or starting; checking too early gives false results.

  3. Why is “supply air high while valve > 90 %” a useful alarm?

    Show answer
    It shows the AHU is asking for full cooling and still failing, pointing to the chilled water supply rather than the AHU.

  4. Name three things a good alarm message contains.

    Show answer
    What is wrong, where (which plant/area), and what to check first or the impact.

  5. Why should temperature alarms have an enable condition?

    Show answer
    So they do not fire when the plant is off by schedule or starting up — when the values are expected to be out of range.
